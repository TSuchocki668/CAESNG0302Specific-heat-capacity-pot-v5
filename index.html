Specific heat capacity in water

incoming heat is shared between disrupting hydrogen bonds and increasing molecular motion, slowing the temperature rise

A thermometer measures average molecular motion. In water, much of the incoming energy is diverted into disrupting hydrogen bonds instead of increasing that motion, so the temperature rises more slowly. In the lower region near the heat source, bonds are being disrupted and molecules begin moving more freely. Higher in the water, more bonds remain intact and molecular motion is slower. This delayed temperature response is what we describe as high specific heat capacity: the ability to absorb large amounts of heat before warming significantly.
